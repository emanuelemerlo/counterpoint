# Choosing a species and style

The **Style** box decides *what kind* of counterpoint you get. Here is what to
choose for the most common situations. For the musical background see
[Counterpoint in a nutshell](Counterpoint-in-a-nutshell).

## Quick recipes

| You have... | Species / rhythm | Position | Rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| a cantus firmus in whole notes, for a counterpoint class | 1:1, 2:1, 4:1, Suspensions or Florid | Above or Below | Strict |
| a song or a theme with its own rhythm | Free | Automatic | Strict, or Free for a more modern sound |
| melody and bass (a hymn, a piano piece) and you want an inner voice | Free | Below the melody, above the bass | Strict |
| a melody for which you want a bass line | Free or 1:1 | Below the melody, as the bass | Strict |
| a melody with rests that you want answered | Free, with «Imitate the melody's motif in its rests» | Automatic | either |
| a theme that should be imitated, as in a canon or a fugue | Free or Florid, with «Imitative entry» | Above or Below | Free allows more intervals |

## Species / rhythm

- **Free (complementary rhythm)**: for real music. The counterpoint moves when
  the melody holds a note and holds when the melody moves, so the two lines
  don't get in each other's way. The rhythm comes from the beats of the bar.
- **1:1 – note against note**: one note for each melody note, only
  consonances. The most "chorale-like" result.
- **2:1 – two notes against one**: two notes for each melody note, the second
  may be a passing or neighbour dissonance.
- **4:1 – four notes against one**: a running line, with passing notes,
  neighbours and the third-species figures (cambiata, double neighbour).
- **Suspensions (4th species)**: the voice is tied across the beat and creates
  suspensions that resolve down by step: expressive and "vocal".
- **Florid (5th species)**: a mixture of all the above with varied rhythms.

The species 1:1, 2:1, 4:1 and suspensions build their rhythm on the melody's
notes, so they work best when the melody is in long, even notes. On a melody
with its own rhythm use Free or Florid.

## Position

- **Automatic**: the plugin decides from where melody and bass are.
- **Above the melody (soprano/alto)**: a descant.
- **Below the melody, above the bass (tenor)**: an inner voice; needs a bass.
- **Below the melody, as the bass**: only when there is no bass.

If the melody goes so high or low that there is no room, the voice crosses to
the other side of the melody for those bars (*voice exchange*) instead of
resting; the description tells you in how many bars.

## Register

**Automatic** follows the melody. **Soprano, Alto, Tenor, Bass** force a vocal
range. When writing to a new staff the instrument's range always wins: no note
leaves it.

## Rules

- **Strict (academic)**: the rules of the textbooks. Dissonances only as
  passing or neighbour notes, prepared suspensions or dominant sevenths;
  hidden fifths and octaves avoided between outer voices; only the ascending
  minor sixth among the sixths.
- **Free**: also appoggiaturas (an accented dissonance that resolves by step),
  chord sevenths, hidden fifths tolerated. More modern, less "exercise-like".

The same choice also applies to **Analyze voice**.

## Mode and meter

Normally **Automatic**: the plugin recognises the key, major or minor, and
simple or compound meter, also with key changes. Force them only if the plugin
gets them wrong (the description says which key it found).

## Imitation

- **Imitate the melody's motif in its rests**: where the melody rests, the
  counterpoint echoes its last motif.
- **Imitative entry (free and florid)**: the counterpoint waits while the
  melody states its opening (usually one bar), then repeats it at the octave,
  fifth or fourth (with free rules also at the third or sixth). The harmony
  stays more important, so the imitation may be partial: the description says
  how many intervals were imitated.

## Ties

**Tie held notes** joins repeated notes with ties. Leave it on: suspensions
need it.
